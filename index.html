About

Where the question comes from

A first-generation college student — born in a village, raised in Suzhou, and trained in Suzhou, Taipei, and Ann Arbor.

“The deepest kind of not knowing is not knowing that you don’t know.”

IHome

I was born in a small village in a border region of China. When I was two, my parents moved our family to Suzhou to make a living. Neither of them had finished middle school. Long before I knew what a university was, they told me I had to go to one: a degree was the road to a good life, and the way out of earning a living the hard, humbling way they did. All over China, parents who have never set foot on a campus say the same words to their children, and they hold the university in something close to reverence. My interest in higher education began there. What is this institution that is supposed to change a family’s fortune?

IICampus

I got in, and found that a university keeps rules no one writes down. In my first weeks at Soochow University, a classmate asked whether I had taken the ACCA yet. I asked what that was; he had already passed two papers. My classmates seemed to know which professors one could approach, which scholarships existed, which certificates counted, and how early to start. I learned those rules late, mostly by missing them, and for a while I could only imitate a way of carrying myself that was not mine. Bourdieu later gave me a word for it — habitus — and the sociology of education turned what I had taken for a personal failing into a pattern, and self-blame into a research question.

IIIA village school

The summer after my second year, I taught at a rural primary school in Henan. One boy, a cheerful troublemaker, told me his dream was to buy a motorcycle after middle school and ride it everywhere. I asked about high school. He asked me what high school was. In his world, higher education did not exist even as an idea. I had made it through the door; he had never seen it. The deepest kind of not knowing, I learned, is not knowing that you don’t know.

IVThree systems

As an exchange student in Taipei, I watched another system keep the same promise by other means. Mainland China sorted students into tiers of institutions; Taiwan opened universities until nearly everyone could enter; the United States built community colleges. Each promised a seat for everyone, and each arrived at a strikingly similar hierarchy. At Michigan’s Marsal Family School of Education, I saw what the alternative can look like: mentors who answered questions I did not know to ask, and research and funding opportunities that made a first-generation student feel seen. That is why I study institutions, and not only the students who pass through them.

02

Research interests

What happens after the door opens?

A child from a village, whose parents never went to college, gets into an ordinary university during the expansion, moves to the city, and takes an office job. Has the family moved up? I think it has: this is what generations hoped for — the firecrackers at the village gate, the extra cup of wine a father allows himself at dinner. But one swallow does not make a spring. The question I keep returning to lies beneath such stories: as the doors open wider, does the invisible thread between where students come from and where they end up grow slack, stay taut, or pull tighter?

The ladder has grown taller. My question is whether the order on it has changed.

Imagine two children born in the same year, one in a village and one in a professor’s home. Before the expansion, the village child leaves school after ninth grade to work, and the professor’s child goes to a key university. After it, the village child earns an ordinary bachelor’s degree, and the professor’s child goes to an elite university and then abroad for graduate school. Both climbed: that is absolute mobility. Whether the distance between them closed is relative mobility — and evidence for the first does not settle the second.

The rules behind the wall

First-generation students · the hidden curriculum

Exam rules are posted on the wall, and rural students who trust them can win by them. Inside the university, many rules move behind the wall: that a professor’s door is meant to be knocked on, which internships count, how early to plan for graduate school. I study who learns these rules, when, and at what cost. I also borrow a test from Kant: would a rule still work if it were posted for everyone to read? One that works only in the dark already looks unjust.

02

Expansion, credentials, and mobility

Massification · stratification · credential inflation

Students carry two kinds of assets into college. Some are visible — scores, admission letters, the diploma four years later — and hard work can earn them. Others are invisible — networks, horizons, a safety net, the knack of reading unwritten rules — and they are mostly inherited. Expansion devalues the first and raises the price of the second. The door opened, but the contest moved: from the ground where poor students can win to the ground where they rarely do.

03

Belonging and student success

Student success · intergroup relations

A sense of belonging shapes how students do and how they feel. I think it is better understood as something students carry into college than as a mindset an intervention installs: a disposition patterned by class, whose value depends on the institution that receives it. I want to know when institutions reward it, and how intergroup dialogue and everyday campus life help build it.

04

The philosophy of higher education

Responsibility · fairness · merit

What does a university owe the students it admits? I separate causing an injustice from permitting a foreseeable one, and I take seriously Kant’s demand that people be treated as ends, never merely as means — including when enrollment is expanded to absorb a weak labor market. I distrust easy answers, too. Moving hidden rules onto the wall trades a concealed inequality for a visible one, and a fairer meritocracy, as Sandel warns, is still a meritocracy. Visible is better. It is a beginning.
