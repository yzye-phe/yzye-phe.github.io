01

Lines of work

Three lines of work, one question

Colleges increasingly answer inequality by opening doors: tutoring and advising offered to anyone who asks, interventions meant to build belonging, a degree that is supposed to speak for itself. My research asks which students walk through those doors — and whether institutions reward them equally when they do.

I draw on Bourdieu’s account of capital and its conversion and on organizational sociology, and I test them with national longitudinal data and measurement that holds across groups. I am most interested in the conditions under which a gap appears, and those under which it disappears.

I

Social origins & conversion

Who turns open opportunities into advantage?

With HSLS:09 and ELS:2002, I study how family capital shapes the use of formal academic support, contact with faculty, the returns to pre-college belonging, and the conversion of a bachelor’s degree into graduate-level work. In each case the question is not only how large the class gap is, but where it concentrates and where it vanishes.

II

Measurement & evaluation

Does an instrument mean the same thing for every group it compares?

Comparisons across groups are only as good as the instruments behind them. I fit factor models, test measurement invariance, design equity-focused evaluations, and write the reports that carry results to decision-makers — for the NSF-funded PROTEUS project and Michigan’s Program on Intergroup Relations. PROTEUS also includes qualitative work on how students exercise agency when they co-design textbook questions.

III

Institutions & diffusion

Why do universities adopt what they adopt — need, peers, or the state?

Trained in U.S., mainland Chinese, and Taiwanese higher education, I study how new programs spread through university systems. A preregistered event-history study of every Chinese bachelor-granting institution traces how AI degrees cascaded down the status hierarchy between 2018 and 2024 — and shows how common peer-effect measures can mislead.

WP 01 Working paper 2026 Theme I

Open Doors, Uneven Knocking: Social Origins and the Use of Academic Support in U.S. Four-Year Colleges

Sole author · HSLS:09 with postsecondary transcripts · N = 7,873 four-year entrants

Colleges have turned the “hidden curriculum” into formal services open to anyone who asks. Yet students at the top of the SES distribution were 27 points more likely than those at the bottom to seek course help, and 18 to 19 points more likely to use academic support and career services — gaps as large as those for faculty research and study abroad. Need-targeted aid advising ran the other way. The gap concentrated among struggling students and off-campus students at less selective institutions, and was absent among residential students and at highly selective ones.

ArgumentConditional activation: formal support equalizes only where institutions lower the cost of initiating contact for the students least likely to initiate it.

Abstract

Colleges address class inequality by formalizing support (tutoring, advising, career offices) available to any student who asks. Using nationally representative data on 7,873 students in the High School Longitudinal Study of 2009 who began at four-year institutions, I estimate socioeconomic gradients in support use through early 2016. Students at the top of the socioeconomic distribution were 27 percentage points more likely than those at the bottom to seek course help and 18 to 19 points more likely to use academic support and career services, gradients as large as those for faculty research and study abroad. A curriculum-embedded experience showed a weaker gradient and need-targeted aid advising a reversed one. Decompositions associate most of the gap with institutional sorting, shorter enrollment, prior help-seeking, and work. The gap was concentrated among academically struggling students and off-campus students at less selective institutions. It was absent among residential students and at highly selective institutions.

  • Help-seeking

WP 02 Working paper 2026 Theme I

Same Degree, Different Doors: Social Origins, College Selectivity, and Graduate-Level Employment

Sole author · ELS:2002 · N = 3,710 bachelor’s degree holders at about age 26

A bachelor’s degree is also a credential, and its value depends on converting it into a job that requires it. Net of pre-college achievement, family SES no longer predicted earnings or professional employment at age 26 — but it still predicted holding a degree-requiring job. That gap was concentrated among graduates who began at highly selective colleges (31 points) and was negligible elsewhere (4 points). Grades, majors, internships, debt, and graduate degrees explained little of it.

ArgumentAdmission to a selective college — the step most often studied as a channel of mobility — is not the last step at which social origins matter.

Abstract

Research on whether a bachelor’s degree equalizes opportunity has focused on earnings and occupational status. This study asks whether graduates from different origins are equally able to use the degree by obtaining jobs that require it. Using data on 3,710 bachelor’s degree holders in the Education Longitudinal Study of 2002 at about age 26, I find that socioeconomic status predicted holding a degree-requiring job (20 percentage points between the bottom and top of the distribution). Net of pre-college achievement, it did not significantly predict earnings or professional employment. The gradient was concentrated among graduates who began at highly selective institutions (31 points) and was negligible elsewhere (4 points). Among selective-college graduates, grades, majors, internships, debt, and graduate degrees explained little of the gap. Lower-SES graduates of these institutions were also less likely to describe their jobs as advancing their careers, which argues against a preference explanation.

  • Credentials
  • College selectivity

WP 03 AERA 2027 · Proposal submitted Theme I Theme II

Belonging as a Class-Patterned Disposition: Conditional Returns to Pre-College Belonging for Postsecondary Persistence

Sole author · HSLS:09 linked to the Postsecondary Education Transcript Study

If belonging is both consequential and readily modifiable, why has cultivating it not narrowed class gaps in persistence? I treat belonging as a disposition students carry into college, patterned by social class, whose value depends on the institution that receives it. The belonging scale proves invariant across SES quintiles, so the class gap in belonging is substantive rather than an artifact of the items. Its returns are conditional: a one-SD increase in ninth-grade belonging is associated with a 2.8-point gain in persistence at selective four-year colleges and no reliable gain elsewhere.

ArgumentThe disposition is measured equivalently across the socioeconomic distribution; what varies is whether the institution rewards it.

Research questions
  1. Does the association between a ninth-grade belonging disposition and later persistence vary by family capital and by institutional field?
  2. Does belonging mediate part of the link between capital and persistence, and does field moderate that pathway?
  3. Is the belonging scale equivalent across social class, or do its items behave differently for lower- and higher-income students?
  • Bourdieu
  • Measurement invariance
  • Average marginal effects
  • E-value
WP 04 Working paper Preregistered Theme III

Who Opens an AI Degree, and When? Status Cascades, Provincial Clustering, and Weak Market Signals in the Diffusion of Artificial Intelligence Programmes across Chinese Universities, 2018–2024

MOE filing & approval records · 1,256 institutions · 6,603 institution-cycles

Between 2018 and 2024, half of China’s roughly 1,250 bachelor-granting institutions filed to open an undergraduate AI program. Discrete-time hazard models on the full population of Ministry of Education records show that adoption cascaded down the status hierarchy, tracked provincial demand for digital labor only weakly, and clustered within provinces through synchronized timing rather than imitation. In the preregistered confirmatory window, only state pilot-zone signals (HR 1.64) and the conversion of independent colleges (HR 2.16) meet the inference rule.

ArgumentA state-scaffolded status cascade, rather than a market response or a simple contagion process.

Abstract

Between 2018 and 2024 half of China’s roughly 1,250 bachelor-granting institutions filed to open an undergraduate programme in Artificial Intelligence (programme code 080717T). We use the complete population of Ministry of Education (MOE) filing and approval records, linked to institutional registers, status lists, provincial industry data and the dates of state AI pilot-zone designations, to estimate discrete-time hazard models of adoption. We test whether adoption followed local demand for digital labour (a market logic), the prior moves of peers (mimetic isomorphism), or state signals, and whether these drivers shifted as diffusion proceeded, as Tolbert and Zucker’s two-stage model predicts. Three findings stand out. First, adoption cascaded down the status hierarchy: world-class universities were about forty times as likely as ordinary public institutions to adopt in the first cycles, but the tier gradient disappeared after 2021, while private institutions and converted independent colleges became disproportionately likely to adopt late. Second, provincial measures of ICT intensity and ICT investment growth have small and statistically fragile associations with adoption in every phase, and the same null holds for three comparison programmes. Third, adoption clusters strongly within provinces (permutation p = .018), but a lead placebo shows that the clustering reflects synchronised provincial timing rather than sequential imitation, and cumulative peer measures at the tier and sector levels are negative because they track depletion of the at-risk pool. In the preregistered confirmatory window (2022–2024) only the pilot-zone signal (hazard ratio 1.64) and the conversion of independent colleges (HR 2.16) meet the inference rule. We read the evidence as a state-scaffolded status cascade rather than a market response or a simple contagion process, and we show how cumulative peer measures can mislead in diffusion studies with heterogeneous populations.

  • Event-history analysis
  • Institutional isomorphism
  • Permutation tests
  • China

Beyond Classroom Engagement: How Student-Initiated Faculty Interactions Shape Academic Success

Ye, Y. · Presenter · ELS:2002, transcript-verified GPA

  1. 01

    A modest, durable dividend. Each step up in out-of-class contact with faculty is associated with 2–3 more GPA percentile points, net of prior achievement and SES.

  2. 02

    The first-contact premium. The jump comes from moving from no contact to any contact; going from “sometimes” to “often” adds about one point.

  3. 03

    Lifts all boats, preserves gaps. The return is the same across SES and race, so contact raises averages without closing achievement gaps.

Program on Intergroup Relations, University of Michigan
